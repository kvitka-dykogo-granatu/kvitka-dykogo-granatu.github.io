X-Raying a Research Institute

Master thesis in the AVIZ team at Inria Saclay, April to September 2026. Supervised by Petra Isenberg and Jean-Daniel Fekete. Best Poster Award, IEEE VIS 2026.

Built with Python, SQL in DuckDB, JavaScript and D3 v7, a locally run language model (Qwen 3.6), NMF topic modelling, uv.

Poster on Zenodo

The managers of the Inria Saclay research centre keep their records in four systems built for other purposes: a team registry, annual activity reports, the national publication archive and the national thesis platform. I merged them into one data warehouse and built a browser tool on top of it, so the managers can explore 76 research teams, their members and their publications from 2008 to 2024. The tool runs behind Inria's login and needs no server.

  • Wrote one parser per source and loaded 4,534 activity reports, 16,208 publication records and 1,077 doctoral theses into a DuckDB warehouse with raw, staging, core and mart layers.
  • Wrote the matching rules for teams, people and institutions in SQL. An author counts as matched only when team membership in the same year confirms the name match: 20,432 confirmed links out of 35,269 candidate pairs. The rule was to under-credit, never to mis-credit.
  • Added 22 assertions, table checksums and a planted-violation test to every build. One command rebuilds everything in a median of 154 seconds, and a build replaces the live warehouse only after every check passes.
  • Compared a local language model (Qwen 3.6) with an NMF topic model for sorting 12,027 publications into the institute's 14 themes. I judged both on three signals neither model had seen, with 95% intervals from 300 bootstrap resamples. The language model's themes matched the teams' declared themes for 61.7% of team–publication pairs, against 47.4% for NMF.
  • Designed and built the interface in D3: a circle-packed overview of the centre, a team view, a comparison of selected teams and a collaboration circle. Every number states the years it covers, and a missing value is shown as missing, never as zero.
  • Ran the requirements study (a survey and a card-sorting workshop with 8 committee members) and the evaluation: both centre managers finished a task from their own work without help, and 5 researchers rated the tool between 5.3 and 5.7 out of 7 on the ICE-T questionnaire.

Fine-grained bird species classification

Class Kaggle competition, Machine Learning course, CentraleSupélec, January to February 2026. Team of two; I built the whole pipeline.

Built with PyTorch, timm, YOLOv8, Grounding DINO, Hugging Face Transformers, Albumentations, OpenCV, scikit-learn, Google Colab.

Recognise 20 bird species from photos, with about 60 photos per species and 1,212 labelled images in total. Some pairs look almost the same, like the American Crow and the Fish Crow. Our team finished 1st on the private leaderboard with 92% accuracy; 2nd place had 88%.

  • Started with baselines to see what matters. A frozen ResNet-50 scored 48%; fine-tuning it gave about 70%.
  • Compared four backbones in the same simple pipeline. EVA-02 Large, a Vision Transformer pretrained on iNaturalist photos of living species, beat ConvNeXt-Base by 4 points. Everything added after it gave 2 more points.
  • Added bird detection (YOLOv8, with Grounding DINO as a fallback for the 13 test photos YOLO missed), 5-fold cross-validation on all 1,212 images (96.95% ± 0.43%) and test-time augmentation with 4 views per photo.
  • Kept a 304-million-parameter model from overfitting on 1,212 images: a tiny learning rate for the backbone, a faster one for the new head, warm-up, label smoothing, Mixup and CutMix, early stopping.
  • Traced 78% of the remaining errors to two look-alike pairs with a confusion matrix, and trained small specialist classifiers that step in only when the main model hesitates between the pair.
  • Ran experiments one change at a time, with the same folds and seed, and logged every run. Dropped weight averaging and model soup when the numbers did not hold up.

Building segmentation on satellite images

Bachelor thesis, Igor Sikorsky Kyiv Polytechnic Institute, February to June 2024. Grade 95 of 100.

Built with PyTorch, segmentation-models-pytorch, Torchvision, Albumentations, OpenCV, scikit-image, scikit-fuzzy, scikit-learn, Kaggle.

Code on GitHub

Find buildings on satellite photos. I compared three methods that need no labels (K-Means, Fuzzy C-Means and morphological active contours) with three pretrained segmentation networks (U-Net, U-Net++ and FPN), on the same photos and with the same metrics.

  • Prepared 8,366 images from the Mapping Challenge dataset with 71,871 building outlines, turned the outlines into pixel masks and split the data 8:1:1 into training, validation and test sets.
  • Turned colour clusters and active-contour outputs into building masks, and scored every method with IoU, Dice and pixel accuracy on the same 837 test images.
  • Fine-tuned U-Net, U-Net++ and FPN with ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50 encoders, with Dice loss, on a Kaggle P100 GPU within 30 free hours a week.
  • The networks reached about 0.80 IoU. The best method without labels reached 0.37, and that figure used the true masks to pick the right cluster, so it is an upper bound.
  • Found later, while reviewing the code, that a modified network with dilated convolutions was never switched on, so its small gain was noise. Since then I check that a change is really in the model before I compare numbers.
